Four model families, the same games

Logistic regression, a GAM, CatBoost and LightGBM, each with location only and with everything (count, pitch, umpire, catcher, IDs). A model beats the baseline only if its paired interval sits entirely left of zero and its calibration is no worse — a rule written down in Week 7.

Platt, isotonic, or neither?

The rule, fixed in advance: the simplest option (none → Platt → isotonic) whose Brier score can't be told apart from the best. Platt only shifts and stretches; isotonic can bend into any rising staircase — and can learn August's noise.

Calibration slice by slice

A model can be right on average and wrong for batters, or in two-strike counts — and the policy makes decisions inside those slices. Zero means the slice is calibrated. With this many slices, about 1 in 20 is off by luck alone.

Challenge Simulator

Set a game state and get back what the optimal policy would do: the probability the call is overturned, the win probability at stake, the cost of spending a challenge now rather than later, and the recommended action.

  • Inputs: inning and half, score, outs, runners, count, challenges remaining, pitch location, and optionally the pitcher, catcher and umpire.
  • Outputs: overturn probability p(s), expected win probability added, future opportunity cost, and challenge-or-hold.
  • What it needs first: the calibrated overturn model (Week 9 — done, see the Overturn Model tab), the win-probability component (Week 10), and the solved policy (Week 11).

Because this page is a static site, the Spring version will ship the solved policy as a lookup table exported from Python, so the simulator runs entirely in the browser with no server to keep awake.

Game Replay

Pick a real game, step to any called pitch, and see what the challenge would have been worth — including the ones nobody challenged. This is where decision regret becomes something you can watch rather than read in a table.

  • Pick a game from the season, then scrub through its challenge opportunities.
  • At each pitch: the location against that batter's ABS zone, whether ABS would have overturned it, and the win probability swing.
  • Decision regret: what the team actually did against what the policy says they should have done, and the win probability it cost.
  • What it needs first: the same three components as the simulator, plus per-game pitch data exported as JSON.

    The overturn model, p(s)

    What it predicts: the chance ABS overturns the umpire's call if the call is challenged. Data: every challenged pitch of 2026 with a known result, split by date — train March–July, calibrate on August, test on September (opened once, after the model was locked). Candidates: logistic regression, a GAM with smooth distance curves per zone edge, CatBoost and LightGBM, each with location only and with everything. Choice: a rule fixed in advance — the simplest model whose validation Brier score can't be told apart from the best, with 95% intervals from resampling whole games. Calibration: none, Platt scaling or isotonic regression, scored by cross-fitting over August games. Lock: the model, calibrator and every file's SHA-256 hash, with pinned library versions and a model card. One correction worth knowing: on challenged pitches Statcast can record the call after review; the pipeline detects this from who challenged and restores the umpire's original call before any model sees it.

    The 2026 ABS rules

    Each team starts a game with two challenges. Only the batter, catcher or pitcher may challenge, and only in the moment after the call. A successful challenge is retained; a failed one is spent. A side that runs out gets one more in each extra inning. Challenges are unavailable while a position player is pitching.

    The ABS zone

    A two-dimensional rectangle over the middle of the plate: 17 inches wide, with the top at 53.5% and the bottom at 27% of the batter's height. If any part of the ball touches it, the pitch is a strike — so distances on this page are measured from the ball's surface, not its centre, and the ball's 1.45-inch radius is already subtracted.

    Challenge opportunities

    A pitch counts as an opportunity when it was taken rather than swung at, the call went against a side that still had a challenge, and no position player was pitching. That is roughly half of all pitches.

    Known limitations

    • Challenges remaining is derived, not published. It is reconstructed by walking each game in order. It has been cross-checked against an independent implementation and spot-checked by hand.
    • Opportunity denominators cannot separate catcher from pitcher. An opportunity belongs to the fielding side; only once a challenge happens do we learn which of them made it. Rates built on opportunities therefore group the two together.
    • Leverage is plate-appearance grain. A challenge happens mid-plate appearance, so leverage here is the state entering the appearance.
    • Listed heights are rounded to the inch, which moves the zone edge by about half an inch for a one-inch error.
    • One season. 2026 is the first MLB year with the challenge system, so there is no prior season to compare against.
